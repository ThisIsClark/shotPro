In a one-motion basketball shot, the shooting arm is still lifting as the elbow begins to straighten into the release. In a two-motion shot, the lift settles near the set point before the main elbow extension. Watch how the two movements overlap in a short video. The form at the top of the shot can look similar in both.

What to look for in slow motion

Choose one shot, then scrub from the moment the ball starts to rise through the release. Use the shooting-side shoulder, elbow, and wrist as landmarks.

  1. Find the lift. Follow the upper arm relative to the torso. Watch the elbow travel upward as the ball approaches the set point. Your body rising in a jump can make the elbow appear to rise in the frame even when the upper arm has settled relative to your torso.
  2. Find the main elbow extension. Step forward until the angle between the upper arm and forearm clearly starts to open. Compare this frame with the two or three around it. That makes it easier to see when the movement starts.
  3. Compare the timing. If the arm is still actively lifting as the elbow opens, the shot looks closer to one-motion. If the lift settles and the elbow then opens into the release, the shot looks closer to two-motion. The transition can happen quickly in either case.

Try watching once at full speed and once in slow motion. At full speed, notice the rhythm. In slow motion, look for the frames that explain what you saw.

What I noticed when I started practicing a one-motion shot

I've been watching the NBA since around 2000, and I play basketball for fun. Shooting has always been my strength on the court. Over the past few years, one-motion shooters like Stephen Curry, Damian Lillard, and Trae Young have helped reshape a league where dominating the paint used to count for more. As I've gotten older, I've also been looking for a way to shoot with less effort. That's what got me interested in trying a one-motion shot.

I used to shoot with a two-motion release. Here are the biggest differences I've felt while practicing one-motion shooting. These are observations from my own shot, and I'd love to hear what other players and coaches think.

Upper body

The traditional two-motion technique I learned emphasized three 90-degree angles, one each at the shoulder, elbow, and wrist. I would hold that shape as I raised the ball, then release. When I practice a one-motion shot, I notice a tighter elbow bend, with an angle under 90 degrees, and a wrist angle greater than 90 degrees.

The order of the movement feels different, too. My forearm moves first, closing the angle at my elbow. Then I lift my elbow, bringing my upper arm and forearm up together. As I release, the angle at my elbow opens again.

Lower body

I bend less at the hips when I shoot in one motion. I focus on how quickly I load and rise back up, and I don't try to get especially low. My old two-motion shot had a more noticeable squat-like phase.

These angles and timing cues describe what I feel in my own shot. When you review your video, start by watching which movements happen together and which happen in sequence.

Film a clip you can judge

Start with a short clip from the shooting-hand side at roughly chest height. Keep the shooting shoulder, elbow, wrist and ball visible from the start of the lift through the release. A fixed phone position makes it easier to separate the movement of the arm from changes in camera angle.

Record two or three shots of the same type and distance. Compare a catch-and-shoot three with another catch-and-shoot three first. Later, you can compare a pull-up or a different distance and see whether the timing changes.

Questions players ask

Can a one-motion shot have a set point?

Yes. The set point is the ball and arm position around the transition into release. In a one-motion shot, the player can pass through that position while the upper arm is still rising and the elbow is beginning to straighten.

Is a two-motion shot a problem?

The label describes how the lift and elbow extension are timed. You can use it as a cue to compare attempts. To decide what to work on, look at how often you reproduce the movement and how it holds up under the conditions you want to improve.

What if the video is hard to read?

If the elbow is hidden, the camera cuts away, or the lift and extension blend together in the available frames, record another clip. FormRefine can report Unclear when the evidence is insufficient. A side-angle retake usually gives you a better view of the arm.
